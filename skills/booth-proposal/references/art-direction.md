# Art direction – think like the creative director of a top studio

This tool belongs to a design company. Design leads what the client sees; constraints shape the thinking underneath (references/frame.md). Read the frame card first, then this file, before you develop ideas and before you write any image prompt.

## How great booth concepts are made

Work in this order, inside the frame. Each step feeds the next; skipping to form produces decoration.

1. **Insight.** What does this client need visitors to feel and remember, and what is true about them that no competitor can claim? Use the audience (who walks past and why), the occasion, the brand's character and its story. One sentence.
2. **Idea.** The insight turned into an image people can grasp in three seconds. One sentence, ideally with a name of two to four words.
3. **Experience.** What happens at 10 m (a landmark that stops people), at 3 m (an invitation to step in) and inside (discovery, then conversation). Include the photo moment: the thing visitors will want to photograph.
4. **Form.** The hero element that carries the idea, and how the rest of the space supports it. Space follows the idea; the layout comes from here, not from a template. Size the idea against the frame at once: a gesture that only works at a size the booth does not have is not an idea for this booth.
5. **Atmosphere.** Light, material, colour and, where it helps, sound and scent, each chosen because it tells the idea, not because it is fashionable.
6. **Graphics and content.** What the booth says, chosen and placed with references/content-strategy.md: one headline, a few proof points, the brand placed with precision, the most important content carried by the hero itself. Typography that belongs to the brand.
7. **Craft.** Proportions, joints, edges, how surfaces meet the floor. The details that separate a studio's work from a contractor's. Name one **signature detail** per concept (a 3 cm shadow gap between bands, a counter floating on a reveal, brass pins on the peaks); it goes into the content map's hero row and, if the idea depends on it, into the render prompt.

## Diverge, then choose

Do not develop the first three ideas you think of. Sketch five or six, one line each, from at least four territories, each with a rough size against the frame. Then choose three from three different territories using the critique questions for Insight, Idea and Specificity and a rough fit. Keep a line on each rejected idea for the appendix; clients and designers like to see the road not taken.

## Design territories

The three concepts must come from three different territories, so the client is choosing between genuinely different experiences. Pick the three that best fit the brand and the brief:

| Territory | What leads | Example of the feeling |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Architectural / sculptural | One bold built form | A monumental gesture you walk through or around |
| Light and atmosphere | Light, translucency, darkness and glow | Stepping into a different mood from the hall |
| Material and craft | A meaningful material, worked with care | Something you want to touch; tactile, specific, crafted |
| Graphic and typographic | Bold graphics, scale, colour fields | A booth that reads like a great poster |
| Media and interaction | Moving image or responsive surfaces used as architecture | The space reacts to you |
| Nature and the organic | Living or organic forms, grown rather than built | Calm, alive, human |

Different territories should also produce different palettes and material families. If two concepts share the same base colour and main material, change one.

### Supporting systems

The hero carries the idea; the supporting system carries the territory. Floor, seating, table, counter and light are chosen by the territory, not left to defaults, so three concepts never share the same chair, floor and colour temperature. Starting points for the concept text (the concept may override any of them; the render prompt does not list them – the image model designs the supporting pieces):

| Territory | Floor | Seating and table | Counter | Light |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Architectural / sculptural | Microcement or large-format stone | Hard and precise: steel frames, leather, honed stone tops | Monolithic, in the hero's material family | Grazing and washing the hero, 3000–3500 K, crisp shadows |
| Light and atmosphere | Dark carpet or dark resin | Soft and low: upholstered, wool, felt | Translucent or self-lit | Low ambient, the hero is the main light source, 2700–3000 K pools |
| Material and craft | Timber, terracotta or stone | Crafted: solid wood, leather, woven | Made of the hero material, hand-finished | Warm 3000 K pools that show texture |
| Graphic and typographic | Coloured rubber or printed floor | Simple shapes in brand colour | A colour block | Flat and even, 4000 K, no drama |
| Media and interaction | Dark neutral | Minimal, kept out of the picture | Dark, integrated with the screens | Screen-driven, cool accents |
| Nature and the organic | Stone, gravel edge, planting | Organic shapes, natural fibre, felt | Stone or a living edge | Daylight-like 4000–5000 K, soft key from one side |

If two concepts end up with the same floor, the same chair and the same light temperature, change one.

### Intensity

Each concept declares its intensity:
- **Restrained**: one or two materials, quiet light, colour only where the idea needs it.
- **Balanced**: confident colour or contrast on the hero, calm everywhere else.
- **Expressive**: darkness, saturated colour, strong contrast or dense material, committed to fully.

The three concepts are not all restrained unless the client's register (style card) asks for it. Restraint must be a choice the idea makes, never the default.

## Principles

- **Use the constraint.** The frame is material, not an obstacle. A shallow stand suggests a façade, a sequence along the aisle or an overhead gesture rather than a deep space; a strict height zone suggests a low, horizontal landmark; a tight program suggests a hero that is also the counter or the screen wall.
- **Specific, not generic.** The concept should belong to this client only. No-swap test: replace the logo with a competitor's; if the booth still works just as well, the idea is too generic. Start again from the insight.
- **One hero.** One element carries the idea at full strength; everything else supports it quietly.
- **Coherence.** Every material, light and graphic choice can be explained by the idea.
- **Restraint serves the idea; it is not a style.** A disciplined palette and few materials usually help. But a concept that calls for richness, colour or immersion should commit to it fully. Minimalism by default is its own cliché.
- **Light is a material.** Design light as atmosphere: washes, glows, grazing light, darkness. Glowing surfaces, light inside a form, a brand colour lit from within – all can be the idea. Light that only outlines edges is decoration.
- **Materials with meaning.** Choose materials because they say something about the client (glass for a vaccine maker's precision, for instance), not from a fashionable list.
- **Brand through form.** Express brand values through shape, light and material; place the logo once with confidence rather than everywhere.
- **Heritage without nostalgia.** Tell history through sequence, material and scale, never through clip art, sepia collages or ornamental frames.

## Clichés (guidance for the concept, not rules for the render)

The old trade-show clichés:
- LED strips outlining edges, neon outlines, RGB light, glowing halos on text
- Saturated gradients, lens flares, high-gloss plastic everywhere
- Several slogans, calligraphy with swooshes, bevelled 3D lettering, the logo on every surface
- Dense text panels, photo collages, roll-up banners, cluttered counters, random hanging light boxes

The new, "tasteful" clichés:
- The interchangeable minimal booth: white walls, pale oak, terrazzo, a cove of light, nothing else
- Generic "premium" materials chosen without a reason
- Abstract shapes or lines of light that could belong to any brand

Use this list when shaping ideas, not to strip the renders: what the client loves in their references (brightness, glow, brand blue, scale) stays unless the idea needs otherwise. Never write it into an image prompt as an avoid list.

## Creative director critique

After developing the three concepts (geometry checked, text written), switch roles: you are a demanding creative director who has seen every booth at every major fair and is hard to impress. Write two or three blunt sentences per concept about what is weak, generic or unresolved. Then score it from 1 to 5:

| Criterion | Question |
| --- | --- |
| Insight | Is it built on something true and specific about this client and audience? |
| Idea | Can a visitor get it in three seconds, and would they remember it tomorrow? |
| Specificity | Does it pass the no-swap test? |
| Emotion | Does it make people feel something, not just inform them? |
| Coherence | Do form, light, material and graphics all serve the idea? |
| Freshness | Would it stand out at a fair full of competent booths? |
| Message | Is it clear what the booth says, with the hero carrying the most important content and most surfaces free of text? |
| Fit | Does the idea work at its real size, inside the envelope, with headroom and walkways? (The geometry check must pass.) |
| Craft | Proportions, details and finishes: would a top studio sign it? |

A concept that scores below 4 on Insight, Idea or Specificity is rebuilt from a new insight, not patched. Other criteria below 4 are revised. Do the same critique on every render. Show the user the critique in one line per concept; the scores only if asked.

Two checks across the set:
- **Supporting systems differ**: no two concepts share floor, seating and light temperature.
- **No empty words**: concept text that leans on 科技感, 高端大气, 打造, "premium feel", "cutting-edge", or "immersive" without saying how, fails Specificity until it names the material, light or form that does the work.

On every render, ask first: would the client say "wow"? Does it look premium, polished and finished, at least as good as the references they gave? Is the idea readable at a glance?
